Breakdown of v1.1 model's performance.

The biggest takeaway here is a highly imbalanced dataset. Out of 3,020 cases in test set, only 178 (about 5.9%) are actual fraud. Given how rare the "positive" class is, v1.1 model is actually performing quite well, though it highlights the classic trade-off between catching fraudsters and annoying honest customers.

1. The Confusion Matrix (The Ground Truth)

This is the most practical way to see exactly what v1.1 model did on the 3,020 test claims:

    True Negatives (2776): Honest customers correctly identified as honest. (Great!)

    True Positives (99): Actual fraudsters successfully caught. (Great!)

    False Positives (66): Honest customers falsely accused of fraud. (These are "false alarms." This wastes investigator time and frustrates legitimate customers).

    False Negatives (79): Actual fraudsters who slipped past the model. (This is direct financial loss to the company).

2. Core Metrics Translated

    Accuracy (0.9520): The model is correct 95.2% of the time. However, because 94% of v1.1's data is non-fraud, a "dumb" model that simply guesses "Not Fraud" every single time would have an accuracy of 94%. This is why we look at the other metrics.

    Precision (0.6000): When v1.1's model raises a red flag and says "Fraud!", it is right 60% of the time. The other 40% of the time, it's a false alarm.

    Recall (0.5562): Out of all the actual fraudulent claims happening in reality, v1.1's model only catches 55.6% of them. It misses the remaining 44.4%.

    F1 Score (0.5773): This is the harmonic mean of Precision and Recall. It shows that v1.1's model is trying to strike a balanced middle ground between minimizing false alarms and maximizing the number of frauds caught.

3. The AUC Metrics (Model Power)

    ROC AUC (0.9432): This is excellent. It means if you pick one random fraudster and one random honest customer, there is a 94.3% chance the model will assign a higher risk score to the fraudster. It proves v1.1's model has successfully learned the underlying patterns of fraud.

    PR AUC (0.6112): Precision-Recall AUC is much more informative for imbalanced data. A random guessing model would score a 0.059 here (since fraud is 5.9% of the data). v1.1's score of 0.6112 means v1.1's model is performing roughly 10 times better than random guessing.

4. The Optimal Threshold (0.3403)

By default, machine learning models classify anything with a probability above 50% (0.50) as a "1".

v1.1's threshold tuning script correctly realized that waiting for a 50% certainty was too strict and would let too much fraud slip by. Instead, it lowered the trigger point to 34.03%. This means the model is instructed to be slightly more aggressive: if it is even 34% suspicious of a claim, it flags it for investigation.
The Business Verdict

v1.1's model is highly capable of distinguishing between legitimate and fraudulent claims (proven by the 0.94 ROC AUC). However, the hard classification results (Precision/Recall) reflect a deliberate business compromise: you are catching a bit more than half of all fraud (55% Recall) while ensuring that v1.1's fraud investigation team isn't completely overwhelmed with false alarms (60% Precision).
